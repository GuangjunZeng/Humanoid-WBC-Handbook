# HuMI: Adapting the Demonstrator to the Robot Before Learning Whole-Body Manipulation

[中文版](../humi-2602.06643v2.md)

Sources: [arXiv:2602.06643v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06643v2) · [official project page](https://humanoid-manipulation-interface.github.io/)

Review scope: the complete 15-page paper and Appendices A–F. The project page advertises code and dataset entries, but no uniquely auditable official repository and commit were available at review time, so this page deliberately avoids invented file or symbol mappings.

> In one sentence: HuMI uses a human-in-the-loop IK preview to turn “a human can do this” into “a G1 can plausibly do this,” then connects a visual high-level diffusion policy to a whole-body low-level tracker through explicit chunk and drift contracts.

Key terms are robot-free demonstration (无机器人示范), human-in-the-loop control (人在环), task space (任务空间), action chunk (动作块), variable-speed playback (变速回放), blind keypoint (盲关键点), proprioception (本体感知), Diffusion Policy (扩散策略), and teacher-student distillation (教师—学生蒸馏). “Robot-free” describes data collection occupancy; it does not remove robot geometry or safety constraints.

## Engineering problem

For a fixed manipulator, hand poses and gripper commands can often specify a task. A humanoid can realize the same hand path by bending, squatting, kneeling, or stepping, and these solutions differ in stability, collision risk, and remaining workspace. Directly scaling human motion also breaks scene geometry: the table, bottle, and sword do not shrink when the robot's arms are shorter.

HuMI leaves task-space geometry unscaled and lets the operator watch a virtual G1's real-time IK result. The operator changes the demonstration while collecting it, rather than discovering after hundreds of samples that the targets were unreachable. The interface functions like a dual-control driving lesson: the human still chooses the route but immediately sees when the target machine cannot make a turn.

The second problem lies at the high/low-level boundary. If a new high-level action chunk is anchored to the lagging measured hand pose, the next target can jump backward. If blind pelvis and foot keypoints are expressed as long-term world targets, open-loop drift accumulates. These are protocol errors, not simply demands for a larger network.

## Core insight

HuMI moves embodiment mismatch into the demonstrator's perception-action loop. A person can rapidly choose a different bend, step, or support pattern after seeing the robot preview, whereas offline optimization must infer “natural and executable” from numerical objectives alone.

It also defines an explicit error contract between layers. Visible hand targets receive local absolute goals and feedback error; unobserved pelvis/foot targets encode changes relative to the current reference. The next chunk uses the previous planned target rather than lagging execution as its anchor. This keeps low-level tracking delay from bending the long-horizon task path.

## Method: input → processing → output

Inputs come from two handheld grippers with GoPros and gripper-width markers, plus five VIVE Ultimate Trackers on the hands, pelvis, and feet. Real-time Mink IK maps five SE(3) targets to G1 joints under self-collision and natural-pose objectives. The operator corrects the motion while watching the virtual robot.

GoPro gyroscope angular velocity is cross-correlated with tracker angular velocity for video-pose synchronization, and ArUco markers recover gripper width. Images, gripper state, and keypoint trajectories train a 5 Hz high-level Diffusion Policy. Full-body IK solutions and keypoints train a 50 Hz low-level tracker using teacher-student learning, speed-adaptive end-effector rewards, and variable-speed playback.

The high level outputs ten future keypoint waypoints over two seconds. The low level combines those goals with proprioceptive history and outputs joint-position targets, executed by the robot's internal PD loop; grippers are controlled separately.

## Control interface and equations

The base tracking reward places position, orientation, linear-velocity, and angular-velocity errors in exponential kernels. End-effector precision is tightened when hands and base move slowly, but relaxed or disabled during fast motion and locomotion to prioritize whole-body stability. Its training weight rises from 0 to 0.5, while minimum position tolerance is progressively reduced from 0.1 m to 0.01 m.

The student observes 25 steps of joint, velocity, IMU, and action history. Commands contain ten future waypoints. This split gives visually anchored errors to a closed loop while restricting unobservable long-term drift to a short horizon.

## Strongest experiment

Figure 6 provides the best causal evidence. Removing human-in-the-loop feasibility preview reduces proposal from 17/20 to 1/10, and removing full-body specification yields 0/10. Removing variable-speed augmentation drops sword drawing from 85% to 50%; anchoring chunks to measured hands reduces throwing from 75% to 40%; absolute world pelvis targets reduce walking cleanup from 75% to 0%.

Figure 7 adds a bounded efficiency result: in one 15-minute protocol with experienced operators, HuMI records 62 trials versus TWIST2's 28, with 96.7% versus 64.3% valid trials. It does not establish a universal threefold labor reduction for novices or all tasks.

## Paper-to-implementation status

The paper specifies Mink/Viser online IK, GoPro/VIVE synchronization, teacher-student DAgger, speed-adaptive reward, Diffusion Policy, ZeroMQ communication, 5 Hz/50 Hz scheduling, and G1 PD execution. At this review point, the official page did not expose a uniquely verifiable GitHub repository, license, and commit.

The correct status is therefore advertised but unverified: no uniquely verifiable official code repository was available. The paper and project page support method and experiment claims, but no third-party repository may be substituted as the official implementation. When official code becomes auditable, the update must separately verify license, commit, training and deployment entry points, configuration, and deviations from the paper.

## Limitations and safety boundary

Author-stated limitations include VIVE dependence on texture and lighting, a low-level controller that is not yet universally cross-task, and validation on one G1. Deployment additionally uses custom grippers, wrist GoPros, an external workstation, wireless communication, and extra pelvis/ground trackers; this is not an onboard-only sensing system.

Independent engineering limitations include experienced-operator bias, small denominators—usually 20 trials and often 10 in ablations—and the fact that an IK preview checks kinematics, not contact dynamics, motor heating, friction, or real gripper collision. Reported 70% generalization comes from 350 demonstrations across seven scenes/objects and 20 tests, not open-world coverage.

This paper does not provide transferable PD, friction, push, or joint safety settings. Hardware reproduction must validate IK, continuity, collision, limits, and low-level stability in simulation first, then use command limits, emergency stop, a separated test zone, and robot-specific review.

## Bounded engineering takeaway

A humanoid robot-free collection system should observe at least hands, pelvis, and feet; render target-robot IK, self-collision, and joint limits during collection; and treat chunk continuity, low-level lag, and blind-keypoint drift as interface-level acceptance criteria. Do not describe the method as robot-independent simply because the physical robot is not occupied during demonstration.

## Reproduction and acceptance checklist

For each demonstration, retain IK feasibility rate, video-pose synchronization error, high-level chunk continuity, low-level hand/pelvis/foot tracking error, and a layered failure label. Separate collection/IK failures, high-level vision/chunk failures, and low-level tracking/contact failures. Without these intermediate measures, a single task-success bit cannot tell whether to collect new data, repair the protocol, or modify the controller.
